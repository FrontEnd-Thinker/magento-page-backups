Link Balancers at a Glance

Intelligent Load Balancing

Link balancers distribute outbound traffic across multiple ISP connections simultaneously using configurable algorithms including round-robin, weighted distribution, session persistence, and application-aware routing. Latency-sensitive traffic like VoIP and video conferencing can be pinned to the lowest-latency link while bulk downloads use available bandwidth across all links, maximizing both performance and bandwidth utilization across your entire WAN connection portfolio.

Automatic WAN Failover

Link balancers monitor the health of each WAN connection continuously using active probes and passive traffic analysis. When a circuit degrades or fails, the appliance reroutes traffic to remaining healthy links in sub-second timeframes, maintaining active sessions without user intervention or dropped VoIP calls. Unlike a single-link firewall where a circuit failure means no internet access, a link balancer keeps the network online until the failed circuit recovers.

Bandwidth Aggregation

Bandwidth aggregation combines the throughput of multiple internet connections into a single logical pipe. A site with two 100 Mbps cable circuits can achieve close to 200 Mbps of effective throughput across concurrent sessions rather than being limited to the faster of the two individual links. Peplink SpeedFusion technology bonds connections at the packet level for true aggregation that improves single-session performance, not just total capacity.

SD-WAN and Cloud Integration

Modern link balancers include SD-WAN capabilities that apply application-aware routing policies to optimize traffic to cloud platforms like Microsoft 365, Salesforce, and AWS. Rather than routing cloud traffic through a central data center hub, SD-WAN sends it directly to the internet from each branch over the best available link, reducing latency and improving cloud application performance without manual policy management at each site.

Link Balancers FAQ

A WAN link balancer is a network appliance that manages multiple internet connections simultaneously, distributing traffic across them for maximum bandwidth utilization and switching to surviving links automatically when one fails. It sits between your firewall and your ISP circuits and is transparent to the rest of the network. The result is a more reliable, higher-capacity internet connection than any single circuit can provide, at a fraction of the cost of dedicated leased-line redundancy.

Failover means keeping a backup link idle until the primary fails, then switching to it. This provides redundancy but wastes the backup circuit's capacity during normal operation. Load balancing uses all available links simultaneously, distributing traffic across them for maximum throughput and still failing over to remaining links when one goes down. Most link balancers support both modes, and the right configuration depends on whether you are paying for both circuits and want to use both actively or keeping one as a cold standby.

Yes. Link balancers are designed to work with heterogeneous WAN connections including cable, fiber, DSL, 4G LTE, 5G cellular, satellite, and dedicated T1 or Ethernet circuits. Using diverse connection types from different ISPs is actually a best practice for resilience, because a fiber cut that takes down one provider typically does not affect a cellular backup on a different carrier's tower. Peplink appliances include built-in cellular modems on many models for exactly this scenario.

SD-WAN (Software-Defined WAN) builds on link balancing by adding application-aware routing intelligence. A basic link balancer distributes traffic based on connection weight and health. An SD-WAN solution identifies specific applications and routes each one over the WAN path that best suits its requirements, sending VoIP over the lowest-latency link, Microsoft 365 traffic directly to the internet, and bulk file transfers across whichever links have spare capacity. SD-WAN also centralizes WAN policy management across multiple sites from a single dashboard.

Yes. A link balancer manages WAN connectivity and traffic distribution but does not provide security inspection, threat prevention, or content filtering. A firewall is still required to protect your network from inbound and outbound threats on all of the WAN links managed by the balancer. In most deployments, the link balancer sits between the ISP circuits and the firewall's WAN interface, presenting a single aggregated connection to the firewall while handling all circuit management invisibly.
